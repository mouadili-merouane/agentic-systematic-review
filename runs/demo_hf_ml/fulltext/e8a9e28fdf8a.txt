Abstract
Background  Heart failure combined with hypertension is a major contributor for elderly patients (≥ 65 years) to 
in-hospital mortality. However, there are very few models to predict in-hospital mortality in such elderly patients. We 
aimed to develop and test an individualized machine learning model to assess risk factors and predict in-hospital 
mortality in in these patients.
Methods  From January 2012 to December 2021, this study collected data on elderly patients with heart failure 
and hypertension from the Chongqing Medical University Medical Data Platform. Least absolute shrinkage and 
the selection operator was used for recognizing key clinical variables. The optimal predictive model was chosen 
among eight machine learning algorithms on the basis of area under curve. SHapley Additive exPlanations and Local 
Interpretable Model-agnostic Explanations was employed to interpret the outcome of the predictive model.
Results  This study ultimately comprised 4647 elderly individuals with hypertension and heart failure. The Random 
Forest model was chosen with the highest area under curve for 0.850 (95% CI 0.789–0.897), high accuracy for 0.738, 
recall 0.837, specificity 0.734 and brier score 0.178. According to SHapley Additive exPlanations results, the most 
related factors for in-hospital mortality in elderly patients with heart failure and hypertension were urea, length of stay, 
neutrophils, albumin and high-density lipoprotein cholesterol.
Conclusions  This study developed eight machine learning models to predict in-hospital mortality in elderly patients 
with hypertension as well as heart failure. Compared to other algorithms, the Random Forest model performed

Background
Heart failure (HF), characterized by impairment of ven­
tricular filling or ejection capacity, is a major contribu­
tor to cardiovascular morbidity and mortality. It poses 
a serious burden on public health and finance, with an 
estimated annual per capita cost of approximately US 
$4406.8 [1]. The Global Burden of Disease study reported 
approximately 56  million individuals globally afflicted 
with HF, 50.3% were women and 69.2% reside in mid­
dle- and low-income countries [2]. Epidemiological data 
reveals a correlation between age and HF incidence, with 
prevalence rates of 3.86% for ages 65 to 79, and 7.55% for 
those 80 years and older [1]. Studies have reported that 
HF is the primary cause of hospitalization in patients 
(≥ 65 years), with 21% of patients being readmitted within 
a month of release [3]. This may be attributed to ageing 
and changes in lifestyle [4]. In addition, there are gender 
disparities in individuals with HF. Hypertension and isch­
emic heart disease are the leading causes in women and 
men, respectively [2].
Globally, hypertension is the most prevalent cardio­
vascular illness, affecting 1.28  billion individuals. In 
industrialized nations, hypertension affects about 60% 
of those over the age of 65 [5]. Previous studies have 
shown that the incidence of hypertension increases with 
age [6–8]. Brent M estimates that the number of patients 
aged 60 ~ 79 with high blood pressure will increase from 
760 million in 2015 to 1,646 million in 2050 [9]. In addi­
tion, the occurrence of hypertension is related to gen­
der, and it is more prevalent in men. However, systolic 
blood pressure rises dramatically after menopause, mak­
ing hypertension more frequent in women (≥ 65 years) 
[10]. Normal aging is characterised by gradual increase 
in blood pressure. When systolic pressure increases, 
diastolic pressure first rises and then falls, resulting in 
increased pulse pressure [11]. This is the result of arte­
rial stiffness and changes in arterial compliance with 
age. These changes foster the development of heart fail­
ure and hypertension in the elderly [12]. Hypertension 
is an important risk predictor for many cardiovascular 
diseases, including heart failure. It is the primary factor 
influencing the onset and progression of HF [13]. Con­
temporary research indicates that 76% of individuals 
with HF exhibit hypertension [14]. Elderly patients with 
hypertension complicated with HF may lead to higher 
adverse events and morbidity [15]. The 5-year mortal­
ity rates for men and women were 76% and 69%, respec­
tively. Moreover, the risk of death increased with age [16]. 
These patients are treated differently than other patients 
with HF. These patients tend to use angiotensin II block­
ers more frequently [17]. Efficient control of hyperten­
sion has been demonstrated to mitigate several heart 
failure-related clinical symptoms and lower the risk of 
cardiovascular events [18].
With the aging of the Chinese population, the increase 
of obesity, diabetes mellitus and coronary heart dis­
ease, the risk factors and in-hospital mortality of elderly 
patients with hypertension combined with HF have 
increased. Therefore, it is crucial to identify the fac­
tors that influence in-hospital mortality and to adopt an 
individualized approach to treatment. Machine learning 
(ML) algorithms have been gradually applied to the pre­
diction of cardiovascular diseases [19–22]. Several ML 
predictive models have been built for assessing mortal­
ity in patients with HF [23, 24]. However, there are few 
studies on prediction models based on real-world data to 
estimate risk factors and in-hospital mortality in elderly 
Chinese patients with HF combined with hypertension. 
In this study, we aimed to develop a ML model to assess 
risk factors and predict in-hospital mortality in elderly 
patients with both hypertension and HF based on Chi­
nese data. Furthermore, to cope with the opaque nature 
of ML algorithms, we apply SHAP and LIME to shed 
light on the predictions made by the most efficient mod­
els [25–27].
Methods
Study population and data source
De-identified electronic health record (EHR) data were 
methodically gathered for this retrospective research 
from the Medical Research Workstation Big Data Plat­
form of Chongqing Medical University. The dataset for 
this study were sourced from six major medical centers 
in Chongqing, including the Second and Third Affiliated 
Hospitals of Chongqing Medical University (CQMU), 
Yongchuan Hospital, University Town Hospital, Tongli­
ang District People’s Hospital and Chongqing Southeast 
Hospital. The study’s cohort included elderly patients 
aged 65 years and above, diagnosed primarily with HF 
between January 1, 2012 and December 31, 2021. Diag­
nosis validation involved a meticulous manual review 
of the International Classification of Diseases, 9th Revi­
sion (ICD-9) and 10th Revision (ICD-10) coding systems. 
Exclusion criteria were rigorously defined to include: 
([1]) patients without hypertension ([2]) patients suffer­
ing from serious liver illness ([3]) patients suffering from 
carcinomas. We excluded variables with more than 30% 
missing values. The analysis commenced from the initial 
significantly better. Our study successfully predicted in-hospital mortality and identified the factors most associated 
with in-hospital mortality.

Data preprocessing and feature selection
In the process of selecting features, we based on the clini­
cal experience and relevant frontier literature on the eti­
ology, pathology and treatment of HF and hypertension. 
Then, combined with the inclusion and exclusion cri­
teria, the final 44 variables were included in our study. 
These variables were integral in establishing models and 
facilitating the process of feature screening. Data acqui­
sition for these variables was systematically carried out 
from the EHR. Our analysis primarily focused on patient 
outcomes, categorizing them into survival or death. 
In addition, we examined demographic characteristics 
such as gender, age, length of hospitalization (LOS) and 
comorbidities such as diabetes, hyperlipidemia, cerebral 
infarction and so on. Some laboratory indices were also 
considered, including N-terminal pro-B-type natriuretic 
peptide (NT-proBNP), neutrophils (Neu), low-density 
lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL cholesterol), lymphocytes 
(LYM), urea, CI, uric acid, leukocytes (LEU), fibrinogen, 
creatinine, albumin, Ca, Na, K, high-density lipoprotein 
cholesterol (HDL cholesterol), hemoglobin, triglyceride 
glucose (TyG) and other variables. Among the variables, 
the TyG index emerged as a novel biomarker, demon­
strating predictive capabilities for diabetes and adverse 
cardiac events. This index is computed applying the for­
mula: Ln [fasting triglycerides (mg/dL) × fasting plasma 
glucose (mg/dL)/2] [29]. Previous studies have shown 
that TyG index was associated with all-cause mortal­
ity in hospitalized patients with HF [30–34]. When TyG 
index increased, all-cause mortality also increased. In 
addition, subgroup analysis showed that TyG index was 
strongly associated with mortality in elderly patients 
(≥ 65) [35]. A study involving 3,026 patients showed a 
1.19-fold increase in the risk of death during hospitaliza­
tion when the TyG index increased by one unit [36]. In 
addition, TyG index is not only positively associated with 
the incidence of HF, but also with an increased incidence 
of hypertension [37]. Furthermore, integrating the TyG 
index into baseline risk models greatly enhances mortal­
ity prediction accuracy [33]. Consequently, the inclusion 
of the TyG index in our analysis was deemed essential for 
this study.
In this study, the proportion of missing data across all 
variables was maintained below 30%. To mitigate the 
impact of these data gaps on the classification process, 
a refined approach was adopted, leveraging a K-Nearest 
Neighbors (KNN) classification algorithm. The KNN 
is generated by finding K samples from the dataset that 
are near to the Euclidean distance of the missing value 
and subsequently utilizing the weighted average to esti­
mate the missing value [38]. Consequently, this approach 
works well when handling datasets with a large per­
centage of missing values [39]. Furthermore, the study 
employed the Least absolute shrinkage and selection 
operator (LASSO) technique to pick out key clinical fac­
tors, while simultaneously discarding extraneous data 
[40]. This approach enhances the prediction precision 
of the ML model and safeguards against overfitting. The 
primary outcome of the research was in-hospital mortal­
ity, which was the patient’s status at discharge, alive or 
dead. Consequently, patients lacking discharge records 
were excluded from the final analysis cohort. These char­
acteristics were then combined into a single dataset for 
subsequent analysis.
Model development, evaluation and interpretation
In this research, we meticulously partitioned the dataset 
into two groups using randomly stratified sampling: a 
training set comprising 70% of the data and a test set with 
the remanent 30%. The primary purpose of the training 
set was to construct ML models, while the test set was 
reserved exclusively for assessing the models’ predic­
tive capabilities. This study focused on the application 
of eight prevalent ML algorithms—Random Forest(RF),

in patients with HF combined with hypertension. Five 
cross-validations were conducted in order to determine 
the optimal hyperparameters for eight ML models. The 
test set is not utilized during model tuning phase. It 
applies merely to model evaluation following the comple­
tion of the model selection and training procedures. The 
training set is preprocessed applying a mix of random 
undersampling and Synthetic Minority Over-sampling 
Technique (SMOTE) oversampling to address the issue of 
positive and negative sample imbalance [41].
In assessing our model’s performance, we employed 
confusion matrix metrics, including accuracy, Receiver

Operating Characteristic (ROC) curve’s Area Under the 
Curve (AUC), recall, specificity, and Brier score. Brier 
score is a measure of the degree of deviation between the 
predicted and actual results. A lower Brier score indicates 
a better performance of the prediction model [42]. By 
constructing ROC curves and comparing AUC values, we 
identified the model with the highest prediction perfor­
mance. Furthermore, feature ranking evaluation serves 
as a method to quantify the significance of individual 
features within a dataset, gauging their influence on the 
final outcome. The contribution of every input variable 
to the model’s output was determined using the Shapley 
value, derived from coalitional game theory [43, 44]. To 
deal with the opaque nature of ML algorithms and facili­
tate clinical interpretation, we applied SHAP and Local 
Interpretable Model-agnostic Explanations (LIME) to 
elucidate predictions made by the most effective model. 
The global SHAP value is plotted as a bar chart to show 
the average importance of each feature. LIME is used to 
analyze the most effective model locally.
Statistical analysis
Patients with HF and hypertension were categorized 
into two groups based on outcomes: survival or death. 
Continuous data adhering to normal distribution was 
depicted by mean ± standard deviation, whereas non-nor­
mally distributed data were depicted using median values 
and interquartile ranges. For statistical analysis, Stu­
dent’s t-test was employed for continuous variables with 
normal distribution and the Mann-Whitney U-test was 
employed for those with skewed distributions. Categori­
cal variables were represented as percentages or frequen­
cies and group comparisons were conducted utilizing the 
chi-squared test. A two-sided p-value of under 0.05 was 
considered as indicative of statistical significance. The 
prediction model was built implementing eight ML algo­
rithms. All analysis and computations were carried out 
with R V4.1.2 and Python V3.7.0.
Results
Baseline characteristics of study population. 
As shown in the flow chart in Fig. 1, we downloaded 
data about 8945 patients from Medical Research Work­
station Big Data Platform of Chongqing Medical Univer­
sity. According to the inclusion and exclusion criteria, 
4647 elderly patients with HF combined with hyperten­
sion entered our study. In the included patients with HF 
and hypertension, there were 3252 patients in the train­
ing set and 1395 patients in the test set. Table 1 shows 
the baseline characteristics of patients aged 65 years and 
older diagnosed with HF concomitant with hyperten­
sion. Specifically, it includes demographic details, labora­
tory indicators, comorbidities, drugs for treatment and 
statistical data. The baseline characteristics are mostly 
balanced and comparable in both the training and test 
sets. In this study, the overall number of survivors was 
4495 and the number of deaths was 152. The number 
of survival and death in the training set is 3143 and 109, 
respectively. The median age was 80 and 79 years, and the 
median LOS was 10 days for both datasets. The endpoint 
event rates were 3.082% and 3.352%, respectively.
Features selected in models
LASSO regression was employed in the training set to 
recognize features automatically (Fig. 2). LASSO regres­
sion effectively reduces the loss function (binomial devia­
tion) by adjusting the regularization coefficient, lambda 
(λ), ultimately yielding a zero coefficient for certain 
variables.
Nine of 44 features were chosen as the best predictive 
to be included in the ML model. These were identified at 
a shrinkage parameter (lambda.1se) of 0.01914052. The 
nine features were age, LOS, Neu, urea, Cl, APTT, LEU, 
albumin and HDL cholesterol.
Development and comparison of prediction models
We developed eight prevalent ML models-RF, CatBoost, 
XGBoost, LightGBM, SVM, Decision Tree, Gradient 
Boosting Classifier and ANN-aimed at predicting inhospital mortality for HF patients with hypertension. 
After tuning the hyperparameters, these ML models were 
trained employing the entire training dataset. Their per­
formance was subsequently evaluated employing the test 
set. To forecast in-hospital mortality, ROC curves were 
generated from the eight ML models. The RF model 
had the highest AUC with 0.850 (95% CI: 0.789–0.897), 
as seen in Fig. 3. The AUCs of the XGboost, CatBoost, 
LightGBM, SVM, Decision Tree, Gradient Boosting Clas­
sifier and ANN models were 0.845 (95% CI: 0.787–0.894), 
0.849 (95% CI: 0.793–0.898), 0.840 (95% CI: 0.788–0.889), 
0.845 (95% CI: 0.783–0.894), 0.718 (95% CI: 0.648–0.781), 
0.841 (95% CI: 0.789–0.889) and 0.807 (95% CI: 0.731–
0.871), respectively (Fig.  3; Supplementary Fig.  1). To 
fully assess the model’s performance, the accuracy, recall, 
specificity and brier score were computed, as indicated 
in Table 2 and Supplementary Table 1. Table 3 shows the 
optimal parameters of the eight ML models for predict­
ing in-hospital mortality. The RF model was ultimately 
chosen to predict in-hospital mortality for patients with 
hypertension and HF because of the highest AUC values. 
Its accuracy, specificity, recall and brier score were 0.738, 
0.734, 0.837 and 0.178, respectively.

Specifically, clinical characteristics such as demo­
graphic features, laboratory indices and comorbidities 
were included. Nine key characteristics were identified. 
Meanwhile, the SHAP values increase the interpretability 
of crucial characteristics. The summary graphs and force 
maps of RF can give clinicians a visual understanding of 
these result. In addition, this study uses more sophisti­
cated ML techniques such as KNN-based missing value 
filling and random undersampling with SMOTE overs­
ampling techniques to handle sample imbalance. The 
results suggest that these techniques are effective.
As shown in Fig. 5, this study identified closely related 
factors for in-hospital mortality. Among them, the risk 
factors were Urea, Neu, LOS, APTT, age and LEU. And 
the protective factors were albumin, HDL cholesterol 
and Cl, respectively. In patients with cardiovascular dis­
ease, the assessment of renal function is critical to prog­
nosis [51]. Urea is commonly used in the assessment of 
renal function as one of the outcome indicators of HF. 
Elevated urea levels indicate an elevated risk of prerenal 
injury, which may be associated with diminished renal 
blood flow due to HF. In this study, urea was considered 
the most significant predictor and in-hospital mortality 
increased with elevated levels.
Previous studies have shown that variables such as age, 
LOS, serum creatinine, body mass index, NT-proBNP 
and diabetes were linked to mortality in patients with HF 
[42, 52–54]. Consistent with previous studies, we found 
that increased age and LOS resulted in higher in-hospital 
mortality. Especially for the elderly, when HF worsens, 
it can lead to severe ischemia, myocardial necrosis and 
finally death. Furthermore, LOS may be utilized during 
the patient’s hospitalization as an indicator of the sever­
ity of HF [55]. A retrospective cohort analysis found that 
an elevated leukocyte count (> 9 × 103/mm3) was an inde­
pendent indicator of in-hospital mortality in HF patients 
[56]. Another study found that leukocyte count, espe­
cially neutrophil count, was an independent indicator of 
myocardial infarction [57]. Recent studies have shown 
that neutrophil-mediated immunothrombotic dysregula­
tion is a key mechanism causing the onset and progres­
sion of HF [58]. Our study found that when the increase 
of leukocytes, especially neutrophils, the in-hospital mor­
tality is increased in patients with HF and hypertension.
Several scholars have found a correlation between 
decreased levels of serum albumin and higher cardiovas­
cular disease death [56, 59]. Israel G et al. also identified 
that decreased albumin was an independent indicator 
of HF mortality [56, 60]. This is in line with our study’s 
findings. Low serum albumin may lead to lower colloid 
osmotic pressure influencing HF symptoms and survival 
status [30]. Findings from 2 big cohorts and a Mende­
lian randomization study indicated that an increased 
TyG index was an independent indicator of HF [61]. A 
systematic review and meta-analysis and several other 
research have found that TyG index is a predictor for 
assessing the onset and prognosis of HF [62, 63]. Based 
on clinical importance, the TyG index was incorporated 
in our study. However, the TyG index was not incorpo­
rated in the final prediction model. The reason is not 
entirely clear, currently. Nevertheless, routine addition 
of the TyG index to clinical models could improve car­
diovascular risk stratification and facilitate individualized 
treatment or preventative strategies [64, 65]. The sample 
of this study was elderly patients with hypertension and 
HF, whose in-hospital mortality rate was 3.27% (n = 152). 
This is lower than the in-hospital death rate of 15.3% 
found by Peng et al. in patients admitted to the ICU [50]. 
Previous studies have shown that patients with HF admit­
ted to the ICU have a much greater in-hospital death rate 
than those admitted to the ward only [66]. According to 
the RO-AHFS registry, the deathrate for ICU patients 
was 17.3%, while the death rate for any hospitalized HF 
patients was 6.5% [67].
Current research has several limitations. First, the 
data utilized was derived from a limited dataset of elec­
tronic medical records from various Chongqing hospi­
tals, which had some missing data. Second, this study 
is missing several important parameters, such as those 
connected to echocardiography. In addition, the data 
were acquired from the patient’s EHR and ultimate 
performance of the predictive model depends on the 
accuracy of the records. Finally, prospective validation 
from outside is required for the model’s future clinical 
applicability.
Conclusions
We construct and test an interpretable ML model to 
evaluate risk factors and predict hospital mortality in 
elderly patients with hypertension and HF based Chinese 
data. Firstly, the model can quickly identify patients with 
higher in-hospital mortality, based on available variables. 
In addition, we identified urea, Neu, LOS, APTT, age 
and LEU as risk factors in the study population. Finally, 
SHAP and LIME is used to interpret predictive models to 
enhance interpretability and utility. This study may pro­
vide a valuable instrument for the identification and early 
intervention of in-hospital mortality in elderly patients 
with HF and hypertension. The study could also help 
increase people’s health awareness and adopt a healthy 
lifestyle. It can also help physicians manage patients more 
efficiently with limited resources, thereby reducing hos­
pitalization and medical costs.
