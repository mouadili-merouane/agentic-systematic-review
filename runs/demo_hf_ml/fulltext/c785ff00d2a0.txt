Abstract
Purpose: Heart failure (HF) discharge planning depends on identifying patients at risk of deterioration or death, yet accurate
prediction from routinely collected electronic health records (EHRs) remains challenging. Methods: We develop trajectory-guided
discharge stratification for heart failure (TGDS-HF), a methodology that reads the patient in-hospital trajectory of diagnoses, vital
signs, laboratories, medications, and procedures end-to-end with a compact short-context autoregressive Transformer, and uses it to
stratify one-year risks of clinical instability (a rehospitalization phenotype) or mortality for discharge care. We instantiate TGDS-HF
on a Swedish HF cohort (N = 42,820) to predict one-year clinical instability or mortality at the initial HF diagnosis in-hospital.
TGDS-HF has three components: category-level tokenization, recency-weighted temporal representation, and sequence model
configuration. We run ablations on these components to show the effectiveness of TGDS-HF. Results: Against traditional eXtreme
gradient boosting machine (XGBoost) and bidirectional encoder representations from Transformers (BERT)-based EHR sequencemodeling baselines, TGDS-HF (Llama backbone) achieved area under the precision-recall curves (AUPRCs) with 95% confidence
intervals of 0.555 (0.535–0.575) and 0.574 (0.550–0.599) across the two tasks at the method default, with robust calibration. A
task-specific refinement using daily aggregation of repeated continuous events improves the mortality task to 0.582 (0.558–0.608).
Further, TGDS-HF maintains strong performance under reduced clinical concept availability and limited training data. Conclusion:
Combined predictions of instability and mortality from TGDS-HF may support personalized discharge planning, ranging from
follow-up in primary care to specialist-led management and, when appropriate, palliative care.
Keywords: Electronic health record; Transformer; Mamba; Heart failure; Mortality prediction; Readmission prediction
1. Introduction
Heart failure (HF) is a chronic, progressive condition with
frequent rehospitalizations and high mortality [1]. During a HF
hospitalization, clinicians face a critical decision: what level of
follow-up does this patient need? Patients identified as high risk
for clinical instability (a phenotype of readmission to hospital)
may benefit from specialized monitoring, whereas those at high
risk of mortality may require tailored intensive care programs
or palliative care referral. Conversely, patients at low risk may
be appropriately managed through routine primary care followup, including annual clinical assessments. Such decisions are
crucial for both appropriate care delivery and healthcare cost
control. Currently, this risk stratification relies largely on clinical judgment, since alternatives such as simple scoring systems
tend to perform poorly for decision making [2, 3]. Accurate,
individualized predictions of one-year clinical instability and

mortality using artificial intelligence (AI) systems could transform discharge planning into a structured, data-driven process
and decision support. However, unreliable predictions place considerable demands and cost on the healthcare system and lead to
suboptimal patient care decisions, motivating the development
of precise AI systems.
Patient health information is commonly stored and collected
in longitudinal electronic health records (EHRs) by healthcare
providers, capturing a patient trajectory through irregular clinical
visits including hospitalizations. An EHR comprises structured
data, e.g. recorded vital signs or assessed diagnoses, and unstructured data, e.g. clinical text notes or medical images [4]. From a
signal processing perspective, EHRs can be understood as multimodal, irregularly sampled time series [5]. In recent years, the
application of AI on EHRs has gained significant research interest due to the growing availability of EHRs and advances in AI
[6, 7]. The field of natural language processing, in particular, has
highlighted its efficiency to learn patient representations from
EHRs [7, 8, 9]. Transformers [10], the backbone architecture of
most large language models, have emerged in several healthcare
arXiv:2511.16839v4 [cs.LG] 5 Aug 2026

applications, primarily clinical prediction tasks for structured
EHRs [11], information extraction from clinical free-text notes
[12] and medical imaging [13].
Traditional deep learning (DL) approaches for EHR sequence
modeling utilized convolutional neural networks [14] or recurrent neural networks (RNNs) [15]. However, RNNs struggle
with long-range dependencies present in EHRs because of vanishing gradients [16]. Although RNN variants like long shortterm memory (LSTM) [17] reduce the gradient problem, its
complexity scales poorly [18]. Transformers, primarily BERT
(bidirectional encoder representations from Transformers) [19],
have demonstrated their ability to capture long-range and interconcept dependencies inherent in EHRs efficiently through the attention mechanism [10], surpassing RNNs [20, 21] and LSTMs
[21, 22, 23].
While efficient, Transformers face quadratic training time
and memory complexity as self-attention learns pairwise contextual representations between all input tokens [10]. The performance of Transformers also scales with the dataset size (tokens) and model size (parameters) [24]. Advanced Transformers (Transformers++) like Llama [25] incorporate architectural
improvements, such as rotatory positional embeddings [26] or
pre-normalization, enabling them to handle longer sequences.
Attention-free designs like Mamba [27] achieve linear scaling
by leveraging state space models (SSMs) equipped with contextaware selection mechanism and hardware-aware computation
[28]. Due to its competitive performance with Transformer++,
Mamba has found widespread applications in DL fields, including language models [29] or computer vision [30]. For
longer EHR sequences, Mamba has been shown to outperform
advanced Transformers [31, 32].
Most previous works focus on general-purpose heterogeneous
cohorts [20, 32, 33]. In contrast, the present analysis focuses
on EHRs from a Swedish cohort with HF, a well-defined and
validated condition [34]. We argue that disease-specific models
tailored around defined diagnostic conditions are more likely to
translate into clinical practice given that the availability and diversity of clinical concepts remain local or lack standardization.
HF is characterized by relatively few hospitalizations during the
early stage, whereas healthcare utilization and hospitalizations
increase substantially during advanced disease [35, 36]. At the
end stage, deterioration is already clinically apparent, limiting
the value of prediction, whereas early prediction can still support
intervention and care planning. Therefore, we focus on the initial
HF diagnosis in-hospital as a clinically meaningful early-stage
milestone. Since these patient trajectories have comparatively
short longitudinal histories, we tailor our study to short-context
settings (≤512 tokens) and tiny- to medium-sized models (2–35
M parameters), reflecting a clinically realistic model deployment
scenario with moderate computational requirements. Although
long-context settings capture more historical information, for
this specific setting we assume that their additional computational cost may not be justified.
Our contributions summarize as follows:
• Trajectory-guided discharge stratification for heart failure (TGDS-HF). We present TGDS-HF, an EHR-based
sequence modeling framework for one-year prediction of
clinical instability and mortality following the initial HF
diagnosis. Rather than introducing a new sequence architecture or representation, TGDS-HF provides a unified
formulation of compact end-to-end patient trajectories from
structured in-hospital EHR, integrating category-level clinical concept tokenization, recency-aware temporal representation through short context, and autoregressive sequence
modeling with a Llama backbone trained on a next token
prediction (NTP) objective.
• Systematic investigation of sequence modeling design
choices. We provide a systematic study of key design
choices that are often confounded in prior EHR sequence
modeling approaches. This analysis characterizes which
components contribute effectively to longitudinal HF trajectory modeling. Specifically, we investigate the contribution
of tokenization granularity, temporal representation strategies, and sequence modeling configurations, including six
model architectures (e.g., BERT, Llama, Mamba), model
size and context length.
• TGDS-HF improves risk prediction over established
EHR baselines. On two one-year prediction tasks (clinical instability and mortality) in a Swedish HF cohort
(N = 42,820), TGDS-HF outperforms a well-established
BERT baseline, with improved calibration. The ablation
study shows that the selected design performs consistently,
with a tiny Llama variant already surpassing larger BERTbased baselines, suggesting that gains arise from architecture choice rather than model scale alone. Internal hospital
validation strategies further demonstrate that TGDS-HF
performs robust across independent clinical sites.
• Translation into four discharge care pathways. Joint
predictions of clinical instability and mortality partition patients into four risk-based discharge pathways, providing an
interpretable foundation for future clinical decision-support
applications.
2. Related work
Transformers, particularly BERT [19], have been widely applied to EHRs for predictive tasks, as demonstrated by models such as BEHRT [20], BRLTM [21], and Med-BERT [33].
CEHR-BERT [22] and ExBEHRT [37] were pretrained on general patient populations and subsequently fine-tuned to predict
30-day readmission in HF subcohorts. Similarly, Med-BERT
[33] was finetuned on patients with both diabetes and HF for
disease prediction and Antikainen et al. [38] predicted 6-month
mortality in cardiac patients using Transformers. By incrementally optimizing BEHRT and applying techniques similar to
those in Transformer++, CORE-BEHRT [39] identified substantial performance gains from enhanced data representations
by incorporating medications and temporal encoding of patient
age. CEHR-BERT [22] enhanced the temporal representation of
EHR by introducing an artificial time token [ATT] which stores
the time gap between two consecutive visits. In CEHR-BERT,
2

different patient representations were compared highlighting
the importance of the [ATT] token, whereas Wornow et al.’s
[32] findings indicate a model-dependent benefit of [ATT]. To
avoid sequence truncation by the context length, ExBEHRT [37]
stacked clinical concepts vertically through additional embeddings. Similarly, Hi-BEHRT [40] introduced a slicing window
mechanism to capture long-term dependencies between each
visit segment. In G-BERT [41], the learned ontology of medical concepts were improved through BERT embeddings combined with a graph neural network. Foresight [42] forecasts
temporally-aware future clinical concepts within a patient trajectory leveraging structured and unstructured EHRs. BEHRT
was integrated with observational causal inference techniques to
estimate treatment effects, such as risk ratios [43].
Recent EHR modeling works have shifted towards architectures beyond BERT, often surpassing it at longer context lengths
[23, 31, 32, 38]. Antikainen et al. [38] demonstrated that XLNet
[44] is a competitive alternative to BERT capturing more positive cases. TransformEHR [23] introduced a new pretraining
objective by forecasting all diagnoses in a single visit using an
encoder–decoder framework. EHRMamba [31] paired Mamba
with multi-task learning and outperformed Transformers at a
context length of 2048. However, the study did not investigate
Transformer++ or Mamba at varying context lengths. Wornow
et al. [32] evaluated sequence models on various benchmark
prediction tasks and EHR properties, such as token repetition
and visit irregularity, showing that Mamba is more robust at
longer context lengths beyond 4k tokens but achieves a reduced
discriminative performance at 1k tokens compared to Transformers++.
3. Methods
3.1. Heart failure cohort
A HF cohort was defined consisting of patients ≥18 years old
at their initial HF diagnosis in-hospital, identified by qualifying
International Classification of Diseases 10th revision (ICD-10)
codes (I110, I130, I132, any I42 or any I50 in any position). The
study period for diagnostic inclusion spanned between January 1,
2015 and December 31, 2022, resulting in N = 42,820 patients.
To include one-year prediction outcomes (Section 3.3) for all
patients, the observation period ended December 31, 2023. The
patient data was extracted from EHRs covering all emergency inhospital and public specialized out-patient care from six hospital
sites within the Region Västra Götaland (VGR) of Sweden (total
population 1.8 million). Supplementary Table S1 shows the
collected clinical concepts including demographics (age, sex,
body mass index (BMI)), diagnoses (DX), vital signs (VIT),
laboratories (LAB), medications (MED) and procedures (PRO).
While DX and MED were assessed by international classification
systems, ICD-10 and Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical (ATC)
respectively, PRO were assessed by the Swedish Classification
of Care Measures (KVÅ1) system.

3.2. TGDS-HF: Trajectory-guided discharge stratification for
heart failure
Structured longitudinal EHRs from real-world hospitalized
HF patients are inherently irregular multivariate time series. Patients have diverse sets of clinical concepts recorded at irregular
time points and frequency, varying across patients and over the
course of individual patient trajectories and ranging from sparse
to dense event trajectories. These EHR characteristics make
sequence models a natural choice for learning latent patient embeddings. We develop TGDS-HF, a methodology that reads the
patient’s recent in-hospital trajectory of diagnoses, vital signs,
laboratories, medications, and procedures end-to-end with a
compact autoregressive sequence model, to stratify the complementary one-year risks of clinical instability and mortality that
drive HF discharge care. The overarching research question is:
RQ How to design a methodology that reads the recent inhospital trajectory of a patient at their first HF diagnosis,
stratified for one-year risks of clinical instability and mortality at discharge?
The EHRs of a patient can be formulated as a sequence of clinical events e j = (cj, vj, tj, xj), where j denotes the event index,
c j the clinical concept, v j the visit index, t j the irregular event
time point and x j the concept-specific event value. TGDS-HF
(Table 1) is defined by three explicit design choices: a categorylevel tokenization (Section 3.2.1), a recency-weighted temporal
representation bounded by a short context (Section 3.2.2), and
a Llama sequence model trained on NTP (Section 3.2.3). Two
separate models with the same configuration produce the predictions for clinical instability and mortality, whose joint outcome
stratifies patients into four discharge care pathways (Section 3.3
and Table 3).
3.2.1. Tokenization (chosen: Vb=10,i=3)
TGDS-HF tokenizes each event value x j, conditioned on its
concept cj, into a shared vocabulary via τ : (c j, xj) →ˆxj. Standardized categorical codes (ICD-10, ATC, KVÅ) are represented
at the category level (i = 3 for ICD-10), and continuous numerical values (VIT, LAB, MED amounts) are discretized into
b = 10 bins. This yields the vocabulary Vb=10,i=3 (Supplementary Table S5), which keeps the embedding space compact while
preserving clinically meaningful resolution. Ablation A1 (Section 4.2.1) varies b ∈{5, 10} and i ∈{3, 4} to verify that finer or
coarser tokenization does not improve over the chosen design.
3.2.2. Temporal representation (chosen: Ht≤C with C = 512)
Patient-specific clinical events are sorted chronologically
from the earliest to the latest timestamp to form a patient sequence E = (e1, . . . , eL), where L denotes the sequence length,
with [ATT] tokens inserted to mark inter-visit gaps. TGDSHF bounds the sequence right-sided by a short context length
C = 512 tokens (Ht≤C), with C ≤L discarding earlier events.
This recency-weighted design emphasizes events that are most
predictive at discharge while remaining computationally compact. Ablation A2 (Section 4.2.2) explores both shorter observation horizons (truncation at t = 0, t ≤1y, t ≤3y) and temporal

3.2.3. Sequence model (chosen: Llama with NTP)
The patient sequence is represented as an embedding vector E ∈RC×dm with E = Eˆx + Ev + Et, where dm denotes
the embedding size, Eˆx the same-sized embeddings of the tokenized values, Ev of the visit indices, and Et of the timestamps.
TGDS-HF uses a Llama backbone [25] (rotary positional embeddings, RMSNorm pre-normalization, SwiGLU activations)
pretrained autoregressively with an NTP objective. The Medium
configuration (hidden dimension dm = 512, 6 hidden layers, 8
attention heads) is the default. The autoregressive backbone with
learnable weights θ maps the embedded sequence E to a latent
patient representation z = fθ(E). Because clinically relevant signals in HF are concentrated within recent activity, short-context
modeling fits the task; Ablation A3 (Section 4.2.3) verifies these
design choices by comparing representative architectures (BERT,
XLNet, ModernBERT, Llama, Mamba, Mamba2), their associated pretraining paradigms (masked language modeling (MLM),
permutation language modeling (PLM), NTP), model sizes, and
context lengths.
3.3. Clinical prediction tasks
A classifier with learnable weights ϕ predicts an outcome ˆy
for a given patient representation z via ˆy = gϕ(z) aligned with
clinical prediction task derived by domain experts. Patient trajectories can be defined at different entry points or endpoints,
influencing clinical decision-making. For example, patient trajectories centered around a first time diagnostic index event
provide different predictive insights and clinical implications
than late-stage patient trajectories with progressed diseases.
Two binary classification tasks were evaluated in the HF cohort at the initial HF diagnosis in-hospital, which defines the
patient trajectory used for prediction (Fig 1a). The trajectory
contains historical patient data up to and including the index
hospitalization for HF. For both tasks, the prediction window
was within one year after discharge from hospital following
the initial HF diagnosis. The first task T1 predicted clinical instability (prevalence PT1 = 39.7%), a phenotype of unplanned
readmission to hospital; the second task T2 predicted mortality
(PT2 = 24.8%). At the initial HF admission, the demographic
profile (Supplementary Table S3) of the HF cohort (N = 42,820,
45.4% women) was median 80 (interquartile range (IQR) 72,
87) years old, with a median of 153 (71, 324) recorded events
per patient trajectory and 2 (1, 3) hospitalizations.
Clinical instability was defined using a rule-based approach
as rehospitalization for a period of at least 48 hours that met one
of the following criteria (Supplementary Table S2): i) admission
to a clinical service unit relevant to HF care (e.g., cardiology
or internal medicine), or ii) admission in a surgical service unit
where a patient underwent a surgical procedure relevant to HF
determined by selected procedure codes (e.g., implantation of
a cardiac device). The minimum duration was set to 48 hours
to exclude hospitalizations which were less severe or possibly
planned (e.g., routine checkups, precautionary in-hospital observation or planned interventions not caused by worsening of
HF).
3.4. Patient sequence representation
To construct a patient sequence, hospitalizations with at least
one diagnosis were chronologically ordered so that the latest
event occurred right-sided. Consecutive hospitalizations less
than 24 hours apart were merged into one. Hospitalizations
that led to in-hospital death were excluded as not relevant for
the outcomes. Out-patient encounters were excluded because
they indicate low clinical severity. The events from all patient
sequences were tokenized and mapped to a shared baseline
vocabulary. ICD-10 and KVÅ were registered at the third codespecific hierarchical level. Related KVÅ codes with a digit in
the third position were standardized by replacing the digit with
an uncommon shared character. New MED concepts were constructed by concatenation of the ATC code (level 4), the route of
administration (e.g., intravenous or oral) and the amount of medication. All VIT, LAB and MED concepts were independently
discretized into 10 quantile bins. No unit normalization was
applied, as all continuous measurements were recorded using
standardized units, while MED were encoded as administration
route-specific concepts, ensuring consistent units. Missing BMI
values were median imputed, as BMI was part of the demographic embeddings and not a concept of the patient trajectory.

3.5. Implementation
This work investigated six sequence models across three
architecture classes: (i) Transformers (BERT [19], XLNet
[44]), (ii) Transformers++ (ModernBERT [46] abbreviated as
MBERT, Llama [25]), and (iii) Mambas (Mamba [27], Mamba2
[47]). Their associated learning objectives are: MLM (BERT,
MBERT), PLM (XLNet), NTP (Llama, Mamba, Mamba2). To
compare with traditional machine learning, the commonly used
eXtreme gradient boosting machine (XGBoost) [48] was trained
on the frequency of clinical concepts within a patient sequence
[22, 31, 37]. Supplementary Section S2.1 describes the architectural variations across the sequence models while Supplementary
Section S2.2 provides a theoretical comparison between Transformer and Mamba. The implementation details, including hardware and software versions, data split, and training procedure,
are outlined in Supplementary Section S2.3.
The prevalence of outcomes in the HF cohort (Supplementary Table S3) is low, e.g., the one-year mortality following the
initial HF diagnosis is 24.8%. Therefore, the area under the
precision-recall curve (AUPRC) was reported as primary metric
for discriminative evaluation instead of the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) to better capture
the model’s ability to identify true positives in the presence of
class imbalance. The calibration capability was assessed using
the Brier score which measures the mean squared error between
predicted probabilities and actual outcomes. All metrics were
reported with 95% confidence intervals (CIs), computed via
bootstrapping (1000 iterations).
4. Experiments and results
We first report the main result addressing the overarching
research question (RQ) (TGDS-HF vs. baselines), followed

of TGDS-HF at a time. Unless otherwise specified, all models
were trained at the TGDS-HF defaults: vocabulary Vb=10,i=3,
cutoff temporal representation Ht≤C, context length C = 512,
and model size S = Medium.
As baselines, we used the
classical XGBoost on bag-of-concept frequencies [22, 31, 37]
and a BERT backbone motivated by the strong performance

Med-BERT, and CEHR-BERT. We further included ModernBERT (MBERT), an improved BERT architecture, and XLNet,
a common BERT alternative, as baselines.
4.1. Main results
Table 2 reports AUPRC for TGDS-HF (Llama, NTP, Ht≤C,
Medium) and the baselines; Supplementary Table S10 repeats
the comparison for AUROC and Brier score. TGDS-HF achieves
AUPRC of 0.555 (T1) and 0.574 (T2), improving over the trivial
XGBoost baseline by +0.036 and +0.049 AUPRC, over MBERT,
the strongest advanced baseline, by +0.021 and +0.017 AUPRC,
with confidence intervals shifting upward across both tasks (Table 2). Calibration (Brier score) is similarly improved (Supplementary Table S10). A task-specific refinement of TGDS-HF using daily aggregation (Ht≤C,w=1d) further improves the mortality
task to AUPRC 0.582 (T2); this refinement is part of Ablation A2
rather than the method default. Tiny variants of Llama and Mambas already surpass the Medium BERT/XLNet/MBERT baselines on both tasks while requiring substantially fewer model
parameters and giga multiply-add operations (GMACs), so the
gain attributable to TGDS-HF cannot be reduced to model size
alone.
4.2. Ablation study
The ablation study perturbs one component of TGDS-HF at
a time, holding the rest at the default (Llama, NTP, Vb=10,i=3,
Ht≤C, C = 512, Medium). The four sub-questions guiding the
study are:
RQ1 How effective is the chosen tokenization Vb=10,i=3 compared
to finer or coarser alternatives across b ∈{5, 10} and i ∈
{3, 4}?
RQ2 How effective is the chosen temporal representation Ht≤C
compared to truncation and aggregation alternatives?
RQ3 How does the sequence-model configuration, including
backbone, model size, and context length, shape the effectiveness on short-context patient trajectories?
RQ4 How gracefully does TGDS-HF degrade under reduced
clinical concept availability and limited training data?
Visualizations of AUROC and Brier score along with all numerical metrics are reported in Supplementary Section S3.
4.2.1. A1: Tokenization (RQ1)
The granularity of the shared event vocabulary is perturbed
in two ways: the discretization resolution of VIT, LAB and
MED varies with b ∈{5, 10}, and the hierarchical ICD-10 code
level varies with i ∈{3, 4}. The category-level encoding (i = 3)
keeps the embedding space compact while preserving clinically
meaningful resolution [37, 39]; the diagnosis level (i = 4, e.g.
I509) provides more precise assessments by potentially identifying detailed phenotypes within the HF cohort, at the cost of a
larger vocabulary. Supplementary Table S5 reports the resulting
vocabulary sizes.
Fig. 2 reports AUPRC for the four combinations, ordered
by increasing unique-token count. For Llama (the TGDS-HF
backbone), the chosen Vb=10,i=3 is at or above the best for T1
across all four vocabularies and competitive on T2; the finest
vocabulary Vb=10,i=4 does not improve over Vb=10,i=3, consistent
with the conjecture that finer encodings add noise rather than
predictive power. Mamba2 shows a similar pattern, while BERT,
XLNet, and MBERT show no generalizable trend, with XLNet
being the most sensitive to the tokenization choice. Supplementary Fig. S1 confirms the same ordering on AUROC and Brier
score. The chosen Vb=10,i=3 in TGDS-HF is therefore supported
by the ablation.

4.2.4. A4: Robustness to data availability (RQ4)
Two robustness studies assess how gracefully TGDS-HF degrades under reduced data availability. Both are run at the TGDSHF defaults (C = 512, Medium, Vb=10,i=3); for the concept
augmentation, concept-specific vocabularies are extracted from
Vb=10,i=3.
Concept augmentation. We vary the available clinical concept
groups by incrementally augmenting DX with VIT, LAB, MED,
and PRO (Fig. 5). The order DX, VIT, LAB, MED, PRO reflects
the accessibility and resource cost of EHR concepts: DX and
VIT are routinely recorded at low cost, whereas LAB, MED and
PRO require integration from external data sources. Concepts
were augmented only within the pre-defined admissions forming
the patient sequence, without introducing additional admissions
or extending the temporal information beyond C. Llama (the
TGDS-HF backbone) holds the best AUPRC across nearly all
task and concept combinations. Adding all five concept groups
benefits all models on T2, while DX+VIT+LAB+MED is competitive on T1, suggesting that PRO contributes primarily noise
on the instability task. TGDS-HF therefore degrades gracefully
as concept availability is reduced; in deployments with fewer
recorded concept groups, the dominant signal is preserved.

constraints. The selection was guided by the principles of (i) covering all sequence architectures and (ii) evaluating each major
ablation in at least two independent configurations. Within these
constraints, the specific architecture-ablation pairings where
chosen pragmatically rather than being optimized. To reduce selection bias, the subset was not restricted to the best-performing
setups identified in the full-cohort experiments.
4.3.2. Validation performance
In SHV, TGDS-HF (Llama) achieves the best AUPRC on
average for the clinical instability prediction and is among the
best sequence models behind XGBoost for the mortality prediction (Supplementary Table S28). The SHV highlights that
XGBoost tends to outperform sequence models in hospitals with
few patients (e.g, H1 or H5), consistent with sequence models
benefiting from larger training cohorts.
Fig. 7 shows the AUPRC values using the CHV strategy for
selected setups, with detailed comparison provided in Supplementary Table S29. Overall, all configurations demonstrated
consistent performance across internal (H1–H4) and held-out
sites (H5 and H6), suggesting robustness across clinical sites.
Specifically, TGDS-HF and its variants remain among the topperforming configurations for both tasks across the three hospital
test sets. Although BERT or Mamba2 with one-day aggregation
perform better than TGDS-HF on T1 for internal sites, they do
not maintain this result in T2, further highlighting the clinical
value of TGDS-HF. BERT and XLNet setups perform worst
with high AUPRC variability, while MBERT ones show taskdependent differences. Mamba configurations achieve consistent
moderate scores regardless of design compared to the larger variability observed in Mamba2.
5. Discussion
5.1. Clinical implications
HF is characterized by impaired cardiac function and reduced quality of life [1], with prevalence increasing steeply
with age and frequently coexisting with, and causally interacting
with, other cardiometabolic disorders and comorbidities [49, 50],
rather than originating from single clinical event. Clinical instability and mortality represent two major adverse outcomes
after the initial HF hospitalization. Their early identification
may enable timely interventions, improve patient’s quality of
life and optimize the allocation of healthcare resources. TGDSHF leverages longitudinal EHR sequences to capture temporal
disease progression for accurate predictions, which provides a
more comprehensive patient representation than static features.
Furthermore, routinely collected EHR data can be utilized with
low infrastructure requirements compared to other modalities,
such as medical imaging or clinical text.
Although related, clinical instability and mortality capture
complementary aspects of HF progression. Clinical instability reflects the risk of recurrent decompensation and healthcare
utilization, whereas mortality reflects the progression toward advanced disease. Combined, these predictions provide a comprehensive characterization of patient risk and enable differentiated
care pathways. TGDS-HF is intended to be a decision-support
tool that complements and not replaces clinical judgment. The
predicted risks may support individualized follow-up planning,
prioritization of healthcare resources, and the selection of appropriate discharge care pathways (Table 3).
Unlike most studies that trained models on general-purpose
cohorts [20, 39, 31, 32] or subsequently fine-tuned for diseasespecific subcohorts [21, 33, 37], TGDS-HF is trained directly on
a disease-specific cohort and captures meaningful relationships
for the two clinically relevant prediction tasks. From a realworld perspective where access to general-purpose cohorts can
be limited, disease-specific modeling is justified as HF-specific
trajectories, clinical concepts and comorbidity patterns may be
underrepresented in general-purpose cohorts.

5.2. Key findings
The overarching RQ is answered affirmatively: TGDS-HF
outperforms both the trivial XGBoost baseline and the advanced
baselines (BERT, XLNet, ModernBERT) across both one-year
tasks, with overlapping or improved calibration. Each of its three
design choices is independently supported by the corresponding
ablation. The category-level tokenization Vb=10,i=3 (Ablation A1)
matches or exceeds finer or coarser vocabularies. The recencyweighted cutoff Ht≤C (Ablation A2) is competitive across tasks,
with daily aggregation reported as a task-specific refinement for
mortality. The NTP-Llama backbone at C = 512 and Medium
size (Ablation A3) outperforms MLM- and PLM-based architectures on both T1 and T2; the Tiny variant already surpasses
larger MLM/PLM baselines, so the gain is attributable to the
design rather than to model size. Finally, TGDS-HF degrades
gracefully under reduced concept availability and training data
(Ablation A4).
5.3. Why each TGDS-HF design choice holds up
Tokenization (A1, Fig. 2). The chosen Vb=10,i=3 is supported
across both tasks: the finest vocabulary Vb=10,i=4 performs worst,
consistent with prior reports that more granular diagnosis and
10

medication codes contribute only minor gains [39] and indicating
that excessive resolution introduces noise rather than predictive
power. BERT and MBERT show poor generalization across
tokenizations, whereas XLNet is most sensitive to the choice.
Temporal representation (A2, Fig. 3). The chosen Ht≤C is
competitive across both tasks. For the T2 mortality task, daily
aggregation (Ht≤C,w=1d) compresses repeated continuous events
into semantically meaningful summaries and provides an additional gain, suggesting temporal dependencies are more important than event quantity. TGDS-HF reports this as a task-specific
refinement: clinicians targeting mortality stratification should
adopt Ht≤C,w=1d, while Ht≤C remains the unified default.
Sequence model, size, and context (A3, Fig. 4). The chosen Llama backbone is supported by the architecture ablation. Tiny Llama and Mambas consistently outperform the best
BERT/XLNet/MBERT configurations at matched C, so the gain
is driven by the contextual learning capability of the NTP objective, not by model size. The autoregressive token generation
may better capture the progression of patient trajectories, as
sequence coherence learned through NTP can preserve clinically
relevant risk signals. Among the NTP models, advanced attention mechanisms appear to learn representations more efficiently
than state-space formulations in short-context settings (≤512
tokens), consistent with prior observation that Llama performs
better than Mamba in similar shorter C ranges [32]. The Small
variant at C = 256 approaches the Medium default, offering a
lightweight deployment option for TGDS-HF. Combined, these
findings support that Llama is effective for short-context EHR
settings.
Robustness (A4, Figs. 5–6). TGDS-HF degrades gracefully
along both data axes: concept diversity dominates training-set
size (Llama at 75% with all concepts beats 100% with fewer
concepts), and adding PRO is not always beneficial, possibly
because the constructed standardization of KVÅ codes fails to
account for hierarchical levels and could be improved with semantic similarity based on code descriptions. TGDS-HF maintains its lead with 25% less data than the advanced baselines
need to match their full-data performance. Further, these findings highlight the importance of diverse clinical concepts and
appropriate architectural choice rather than a large dataset.
5.4. Robustness across hospital settings
TGDS-HF demonstrated robustness across both SHV (Supplementary Table S28) and CHV (Fig. 7) strategies, aligning with
the main findings observed in the full-cohort study (Table 2).
The CHV analysis further suggests that differences between configurations are primarily architecture-dependent and secondarily
configuration-specific, with greater variability found in architectures trained on objectives other than NTP. Consequently,
selecting a sequence architecture appropriate for the clinical cohort and prediction tasks appears more important than extensive
optimization of individual design choices.
5.5. Limitations and future work
There are several limitations in our work. Firstly, TGDS-HF
was developed and evaluated on a Swedish HF cohort for a specific clinical milestone with internal hospital but no external
validation; although HF represents a heterogeneous population,
the absolute performance may not transfer to other healthcare
systems. Secondly, laboratories and medications were selectively curated for HF, which potentially limits the model from
learning out-of-domain knowledge. Alternative patient embeddings, such as the pre-concatenation of demographic information
into the sequence instead of separate embeddings, were not explored. Thirdly, TGDS-HF establishes a predictive foundation
for future decision-support applications; however, its clinical
utility and implementation into clinical workflows have not yet
been evaluated. Prospective studies involving clinicians and realworld workflow integration are required to determine whether
TGDS-HF predictions can improve clinical decision-making.
Future work should fuse TGDS-HF token sequences with multimodal EHR signals, such as cardiac images or clinical text
notes, to explore additional performance benefits. The inclusion
of information for medical disciplines and out-of-hospital visits
could prospectively enrich the trajectory representation used in
TGDS-HF.
6. Conclusion
We presented TGDS-HF, a methodology for trajectory-guided
discharge stratification in HF that transforms structured EHRs
into compact, recency-weighted patient sequences modeled by
a short-context Llama backbone. On a Swedish HF cohort
(N = 42,820), TGDS-HF outperforms a trivial XGBoost baseline, advanced baselines such as the well-known BERT architecture for EHR sequence modeling, and Mamba backbones
on the two one-year prediction tasks (clinical instability and
mortality after the initial HF diagnosis), with robust calibration.
Component-by-component ablations support each design choice
in TGDS-HF: category-level tokenization, the recency-weighted
short-context representation, and the Llama backbone are each
independently validated. Tiny variants already surpass larger
advanced baselines, highlighting the importance of an NTP objective over model size. TGDS-HF also degrades gracefully
under reduced clinical-concept availability and training data.
The joint clinical instability and mortality predictions provide
a framework for stratifying patients into four clinically meaningful risk profiles rather than standalone risk scores, which
may support future discharge planning and personalized care
pathways pending prospective clinical evaluation.

ModernBERT (hereafter referred to as MBERT) and Llama include several improvements over BERT. The absolute position
embedding is replaced with rotary position embedding (RoPE)
[51] capturing both the absolute position of tokens and the relative distance between tokens through rotation encoding. MBERT
replaces the Gaussian error linear unit (GeLU) activation function with Gaussian error gated linear unit (GeGLU) and Llama
with a combination of GLU and Swish activation (SwiGLU) due
to the apparent performance gains [52]. The post-normalization
of the input after passing through a Transformer sub-layer is replaced with pre-normalization to improve training stability [53].
Llama replaces LayerNorm with root mean square layer normalization (RMSNorm) [54] reducing the computational burden by
eliminating the mean calculation required in LayerNorm. For
better representation learning MBERT introduces an alternating
mechanism between global and local attention every three layers
[55].
Mambas like Llama are pre-trained on the next token prediction (NTP) objective, where the next token is predicted based on
only past tokens. Unlike Transformers where the attention mechanism causes quadratic training time, the Mamba architecture
combines discretized state space model (SSM) with a contextaware selection mechanism that parameterizes the SSM to learn
relevant context while hardware-aware algorithms enable linear
scaling with sequence length [27]. Mamba2 improves Mamba’s
efficiency by introducing the state space duality which connects
SSM with attention heads and simplifies the structure of the
matrix A in the state equation of SSM into a scalar multiple of
the identity [47]. Both Mambas utilize a sigmoid linear unit
(SiLU) activation function and RMSNorm as pre-normalization.

S2.3. Implementation
For each clinical task, the data was divided into a development
(85%) and testing (15%) set stratified by the outcome of interest.
The development set was further split into a training (90%) and
validation set (10%). No patient appeared in more than one
set. Here, the development set was used for both pre-training
the sequence model on a model-specific learning objective and
fine-tuning on the relevant clinical task. Patients were consistently assigned to the same set and split across both stages. We
motivate this choice by our pre-selection of only HF patients
as opposed to other, larger cohorts with more general clinical
profiles [20, 33, 22, 37].
Our code implementation is adapted from [31]. Code was implemented using Python 3.10.12, Ubuntu 22.04, and the following major packages: torch 2.2.0, transformers 4.49.0, mambassm 2.2.2, causal-conv1d 1.4.0, and xgboost 2.0.0. Data processing used 32 INTEL(R) XEON(R) PLATINUM 8568Y+ CPUs.
All sequence models were trained on a single NVIDIA H200
with 50% GPU resource allocation (∼70 GB memory) and the
same patient embeddings. Pre-training was set to 30 epochs
with a patience of 5 epochs, fine-tuning to 10 with a patience
of 2 epochs. In both stages, the optimizer choice was AdamW
[56] with a learning rate of 5 · 10−5 and a batch size of 32. In
all dropout layers, the probability was set to 0.1. The MLM
objective was masked with a probability of 0.15 in BERT and
MBERT. For both Mambas, the size of the space state vector
was set to 16, the size of the convolutional kernel to 4 and the
Mamba block expansion to 2 matching the size of Transformer
blocks [27]. EXtreme gradient boosting machine (XGBoost)
was trained using the default parameters (a total of 100 trees, a
maximum depth of 6 per tree, learning rate of 0.3). During finetuning, predicted probabilities were obtained through a two-layer
feedforward classification head applied on the last hidden state
of the rightmost non-padded token, and the backbone model was
further trained using binary cross-entropy loss.
